Built for Simulation, Not Visualization

Choosing simulation fidelity for the decision you need to make.

July 2026 · Tsung-Yen Tsai

Simulation has followed me through most of my engineering work: mobile robot deployments, warehouse automation, storage systems, and later industrial planning workflows. Its form kept changing — Unity testbeds, discrete-event models, Isaac Sim environments, optimization pipelines — but one question kept coming back. Not: how realistic can we make it? But: what decision is this simulation supposed to support?

Early in my career, we used a Unity-based AMR simulation as our shared system testbed. On most days it simulated a handful of robots on a single machine. For the largest integration tests, we spread it across six machines, so the full spatial runtime could keep up with the whole fleet. That scaling took real effort — simplified agents, trimmed scenarios, an efficient communication protocol, failover handling — and for some questions it earned its keep: large-scale stability tests, long reliability runs.

But the question we actually had at that moment was different: what fleet sizes, mission mixes, and environment layouts the fleet management system had to support. The testbed could expose large-scale integration failures, but its full spatial runtime was the wrong fidelity layer for systematically sweeping those variables and isolating the FMS limits.

I have also seen the opposite extreme. If one AMR moves 10 pallets per hour, will five AMRs move 50? Static estimation like this misses queues, contention, and interference — the very things that decide real throughput.

An overbuilt representation and an oversimplified one fail the same way: the representation was chosen before the decision was defined.

Fidelity follows the question

A model describes a problem — objectives, constraints, state — and a simulation lets you re-run that problem under changing conditions, because the real world is too slow, too expensive, and sometimes too dangerous to iterate in. The value of the whole exercise depends on choosing the right abstraction: the one that makes your problem testable, comparable, and decision-ready.

"Right" does not mean "maximal", and fidelity is not a single axis. A rough capacity question may only need static estimation. A queueing and contention question may need discrete-event simulation. A deployment-risk question may need runtime behavior and an error model. A construction or routing question may need 3D geometry and spatial constraints. A robot learning to open a spring-loaded door may need faithful contact physics. A validation question may need structured scenarios and ground-truth outputs.

These are not levels of maturity. They are different abstractions for different decisions. A photorealistic demo can align a room, but it rarely identifies a bottleneck.

That testbed was never three different simulators. It was one Unity-based system, reconfigured as the questions changed. Only later did I realize that we were effectively running it in three modes:

  • Robot behavior. For customized docking, unexpected obstacles, and movement errors that accumulated into docking failures, we adapted the full AMR stack into simulation, including SLAM and navigation.
  • Field throughput. The full stack introduced noise that was irrelevant to the system-level question. We replaced localization and navigation with ground truth from the engine, then used the testbed to reproduce task flow, station timing, third-party interactions, and system-level bottlenecks.
  • System limits and reliability. We replaced the local planner and action execution with lightweight mock agents. This allowed us to simulate 100+ AMRs in one site, run multi-day 24/7 reliability tests, and expose scale-related issues that smaller tests could not reveal.

Same system, three questions, three different abstractions.

Later work on industrial planning and digital-twin problems showed me the same pattern outside robotics: the domain may change, but without a decision, fidelity has no direction. Two earlier projects made the contrast especially clear — one where I removed reality on purpose, and one where reality pushed back.

Case 1: removing detail to get a cleaner signal

We were building a high-density stacked storage system, and the hardware was not ready. The team was focused on delivering the first version of the software, but decisions could not wait: storage, retrieval, and order logic all needed algorithms, and the algorithms needed to be compared.

We started with a spreadsheet — estimate throughput from robot count, layout, order sequence. It was too coarse: it could not capture stack contention, reshuffle cost, or the coupling between orders and containers. We also had a visualization system that showed the whole operation, but it was far too slow for iterating strategies across the system.

Discrete-event simulation became the reasonable choice — because I was not trying to simulate the warehouse. I was trying to compare allocation decisions. So I built a deliberately simpler model: orders, inventory, container slots, stack locking, reshuffle, moveback, workstation flow. And I left one thing out on purpose — the full low-level traffic-control behavior of the robots.

The obvious question: without full traffic behavior, won't the results be inaccurate? Two reasons convinced me. First, reproducing low-level traffic-control behavior at DES granularity would have made the simulator slower and less focused than the decision required. Second, the system-level bottleneck lived in task and shaft allocation and locking — the fleet controller was a separate optimization problem with its own boundary. Dropping traffic was not a shortcut. It was where I drew the boundary.

Each randomized case ran in about five seconds, so comparing strategies — and combinations of strategies — across fifty random seeds became routine. Decisions moved from opinions to means and standard deviations. Some findings were counterintuitive: in our target layout, a simple nearest-location heuristic beat the clever-looking min-empty-slots one.

One boundary we never crossed: this DES was a comparative decision tool, not a throughput guarantee. There was no field data to calibrate against. What it gave us was a stable testbed for iterating a family of storage, retrieval, reshuffle, and batching strategies.

Case 2: preserving behavior because the question was risk

The other half of the lesson came from the AMR deployment — the same system behind the three tiers.

In simulation, robots reached their target points perfectly. So when two robots worked at close waypoints, the proximity-stop behavior never triggered. On real hardware, positioning and movement error triggered it constantly. Our simulator could not reproduce this class of problem — not without injecting uncertainty into the simulated robots.

We fixed it in the real system: adjusted waypoints, widened traffic buffers, enlarged robot tolerance. In effect, we handled the error model in the system design, even though the simulator had abstracted it away. The reality we needed to preserve was not visual realism. It was the error model.

That deployment taught us a stable cycle. First, reproduce the task flow from field logs, and calibrate the simulation against throughput and sensor data. Then freeze the scenario and test improvement plans one by one: tune the layout, change route weighting, reorder station assignments, adjust speeds. Through this loop, throughput improved by 40%+ in one panel-manufacturing deployment.

In the storage project I removed detail to make algorithms comparable. In the AMR deployment I preserved runtime behavior, because the question was risk.

Four questions before you build one

Today, AI-assisted tools make detailed 3D scenes easier than ever to build. But a 3D scene is not a simulation system unless it can be used to ask and answer engineering questions. That requires structured representation, configurable scenarios, controllable behavior, validation hooks, and comparable outputs.

So before building a simulation, I now answer four questions first:

  1. What decision should this simulation support?
  2. What reality must be preserved for that decision?
  3. What can be safely abstracted away?
  4. What evidence would make the result trustworthy enough to act on?

After all these projects, I no longer treat fidelity as the goal. A simulation is useful when it preserves the reality that matters for a decision and discards the rest deliberately. Fidelity is a cost, not a virtue. Spend it where the decision lives.
